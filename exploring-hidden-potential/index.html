Exploring ones hidden potential

May 11, 2024 · 12 mins read

In his book Hidden Potential, Adam Grant, delves into how we can all apply some basic principles to unlock our hidden potential. He breaks down the requirements of exploring ones hidden potential into 3 main constituents - character skills, motivation and opportunities. Before we get into it, I really loved how he introduced the idea of hidden potential by describing our tendency to judge people by their early attempts. In reality, there is very little differences in the long term potential of people who are ahead in the beginning and those that fall behind. This should allay any fears we have about something being “not our thing” just because we struggle early on. Now, let’s get into it.

Developing Character Skills

Personality is our pre disposition to feel, act and think in a certain way while character is the ability to override that thinking and act according to your principles. Character skills are not moral codes but a set of skills that allow us to adhere to our core principles like being pro social, disciplined, resilient, etc. In the long run the early cognitive advantages dissipate while character skills continue to remain decisive. Character skills also propel us to higher levels and prepare us for self growth. Character skills don’t set like plaster, they retains their plasticity and one can update these skills and principles with age. Finally, character doesn’t develop in ease and quiet. It requires practice, failing and experience. Three main skills that get one on the path of growth are as follows.

  1. Discomfort is growth - Learning requires discomfort and it doesn’t come naturally to us. This mindset is a great way to get into learning new things as it helps avoid the instant gratification monkey. Learning is often about finding the right way to learn as per the task (and not as per our preferences). Getting good at something often requires us to apply knowledge before it’s complete or great. This is a type of courage that comes in handy to aid learning. Another courage is to be ok with making repeated mistakes in the pursuit of proficiency.
  2. Being a sponge - Boosting the return in effort for learning requires a “sponge like” behaviour for absorbing new information. The absorptive capacity is the ability to acquire, assimilate and apply new knowledge and it depends on two things - how one acquires new information, i.e. does one react to everything or does one actively seek out information and if one is eager to boost ones ego or get to the growth. What we need for growth are coaches, not critics or cheerleaders but the coaching we need depends on our level. As a novice we need encouragement (so we invest more time and energy) but as an expert we need critical analysis and information, not validation.
  3. Balance of Imperfection - The sweet spot between flawed and flawless is where we shine the best. Perfectionism is striving for zero flaws and makes one hide ones scars and insecurities. As a result of us seeking perfection in our kids, they often judge their worthiness by the lack of their flaws and beat themselves up. Being ashamed of ones mistakes makes it harder to learn from them. As Brené Brown says, what we all need is, to being our wholeselves to wherever we go. Mistakes, scars and all, so we can learn and grow.

Finding Motivation Everyday

It is almost impossible to stay motivated everyday. We need structures or “scaffolds” that help us when we’re down and to remind us what we’re in it for. Here are some strategies to help us build the resilience and ignite the motivation when the going gets tough.

  1. Infusing passion in the daily grind - Being able to enjoy the process makes it incredibly easy to stay in it even when the chips are down. There is such a thing as “boreout” which is feeling emotionally dead on being under stimulated. Just like burnout, where one exhausts oneself on being overloaded, the monotony of getting up and doing it all over, can drain the passion out of things and make it a chore. “Harmonious passion” is being excited to practice and not practising under the compulsion. Much like Deliberate Practice that allows one to hone a particular skill by continuously improving oneself thro challenge, “deliberate play” is doing so by including novelty and variety. This allows us to experience harmonious passion from our daily work. Limiting daily practice or interleaving with breaks can ensure a steady stream of day dreaming which directly lead to creative ideas and improves recall.
  2. Roundabout route to success - Progress is almost always winding and one has to climb up and down many mountains to finally reach the summit. New strategies have to be learned to make progress and that often, in the moment, feels like going backwards. Finding the right coach is not necessarily about finding the best expert. It is about finding someone who understands how you as a novice feel. Languishing is worse than being stuck as it sucks away motivation and to fix that, you need to block out anything that sucks out your energy. By simply moving away and taking a break, or indulging in a hobby or art or a side hustle, can help us get back on the path with motivation. Remember, its a winding marathon, not a straight sprint, and it is ok to take breaks and detours.

The Golden 13

  1. Support network - Nurturing a growth mindset has to go hand in hand with the right support network who sees your potential. Sometimes one need to create their own scaffolding using the resources one has and it usually involves giving and taking help from one’s peers or listening to the advise we give to others. Sometimes being an underdog or having too many doubts on one’s own abilities can make you more resilient. When someone ignorant doubts you, it feels like a challenge and spurs us on. If you have a larger goal for your group or community, your resilience increases and acts as a kind of scaffolding. The story of the Golden 13, a group of African American servicemen in 1943, demonstrated the profound impact of nurturing a growth mindset amid systemic adversity. Faced with severe racial discrimination and skepticism about their capabilities, these men were underdogs in a segregated U.S. Navy. Rather than succumbing to doubt and external limitations, they constructed their own psychological and communal scaffolding to bolster their chances of success. The Golden 13 formed study groups, shared insights, and offered mutual encouragement, embodying the essence of a growth mindset. This self-made scaffolding directly led to their eventual success in becoming the first African American officers in the U.S. Navy.

Setting Up Opportunities

Something as trivial as our zip code can have a major effect on our future earnings. Opportunities are the third main piece of exploring one’s hidden potential. Here are some ways to help people get those opportunities or find them ourselves.

  1. Pillars of learning - According to organisational psychology, practices, values and underlying assumptions are the three main pillars of learning. By putting a lot of value on teaching, even calling it a nation building service and mandated the practice of a masters degree for every teacher, Finland, set up reforms that created schools of opportunity with the underlying assumption that every student has potential. Teachers themselves believe that each student’s learning plan is tailored and focus on personalised learning. Keeping same teacher for the students for upto 6 years is called looping and helps teachers act more like a coach who specialises on particular students. This is a great way to ensure that the students get the right opportunities to learn and grow.
  2. Outlet for great ideas - We should strive to maximise a groups intelligence by setting up processes whereby good ideas come to the surface. It requires more than bringing experts together, it requires a set of practices that unearths the best thinking from everyone. The best teams have the best team players or put differently, have the best pro social collaboration skills. The best team players prioritise the needs of the team and encourage ideas from all. They also have the ability to disagree without being disagreeable. The best teams also have a high level of psychological safety where people feel safe to take risks and be vulnerable in front of each other. This is a great way to ensure that the best ideas come to the surface and are acted upon.
  3. Discovering uncut gems - We all know hiring is hard but kind of make it hard for ourselves. We are constantly looking for people with certain abilities but what we need to do is differentiate between lack of ability and presence of adversity. Finding uncut gems requires us to evaluate our hiring or interviewing techniques to see if they can grow in the new role (rather than focussing only on questioning them about their previous ones). Relying on past credentials works well if people are being hired to do exactly what they’ve done before (which rarely is the case in todays knowledge economy), for all other jobs we need to understand the sort of adversity people fought to reach where they have reached.
